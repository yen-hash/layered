# Singapore lawyer portal is feasible, narrowly

A Layered-style portal for Singapore lawyers is feasible, but only as a flat-fee advertising directory with verified listings, not as the pay-per-lead marketplace that renovation portals use. Lawyers may advertise in Singapore, but they are bound by Part 5 of the Legal Profession (Professional Conduct) Rules 2015 (PCR). The PCR bar touting and misleading or undignified publicity. They also forbid a lawyer from paying or rewarding anyone for a referral, and the Law Society has said that joining an internet referral scheme that charges fees or shares fees on referred work "would be a breach of the LPA" ([Law Society GN 6.1.1](https://law-society-singapore-prod.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/2020/03/121.-Ethics-and-Information-Technology-GN-6.1.1.pdf)). That makes per-lead, per-matter and revenue-share pricing very likely unusable. A flat subscription is not expressly addressed in anything the researchers read, and the Law Society itself sells paid "featured" listings, so that is the defensible model. Demand looks real but the market is crowded. Singapore recorded **7,382 divorces in 2024** and about **43,000 HDB and private resales** a year. There are about 1,150 law practices, roughly 80% of them with 1-5 lawyers, and at least six directories or matchers already operate. This verdict rests on a thin evidence base. Most sources were search snippets, the primary rule text was never read, and the touting and referral analysis is an inference. It must be confirmed by a Singapore lawyer or the Law Society before any launch.

## Lawyers may advertise, but referral payments and unfair attraction of work are the danger

Publicity by Singapore lawyers is permitted, including internet publicity, and sits under Part 5 of the PCR 2015, not the "Part 7" the original brief assumed ([GN 6.1.1 para 30](https://law-society-singapore-prod.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/2020/03/121.-Ethics-and-Information-Technology-GN-6.1.1.pdf)). The Law Society's practice direction on advertising says publicity must not diminish public confidence in the profession or be unbefitting its dignity ([PD 6.2.1](https://law-society-singapore-prod.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/2020/03/66.-Advertisement-and-Media-Publicity-PD-6.2.1.pdf)). The Ethics Committee treats an advertisement as misleading if it makes a material misrepresentation (for example, calling a firm a "leading family law practice" without expertise), omits a material fact, contains unverifiable information, or creates unjustified expectations of results. It treats suggesting that other practices overcharge, or publishing price lists, as undignified (same source). Press and television advertisements are not touting because they lack direct in-person solicitation (same source). A law practice website may carry advertising or generic legal information, but giving legal advice online brings extra obligations ([GN 6.1.1 para 31](https://law-society-singapore-prod.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/2020/03/121.-Ethics-and-Information-Technology-GN-6.1.1.pdf)). A law practice must also keep a physical place of business, so a portal can never itself be the lawyer's "practice" (GN paras 25-26).

The referral rules matter more for a portal's business model. Under PCR r 39(2), when a client is referred by a third party, the lawyer must not accept a commission, referral fee or other consideration from the referrer, must not reward the referrer by any such payment, and must communicate directly with the client to obtain or confirm instructions. The lawyer must not accept instructions from the third party or leave blank Warrants to Act with it ([Law Society PD 7.4.3](https://law-society-singapore-prod.s3.ap-southeast-1.amazonaws.com/2020/03/35.-Warrant-To-Act-Letter-of-Engagement-and-Referrals-from-Third-Parties-PD-7.4.3.pdf)). The Legal Profession Act (LPA) adds that it is improper for a solicitor to give any gratification for procuring employment, or to procure work through someone who was promised remuneration for obtaining it (s 83(2)(d) and (e), as cited in GN 6.1.1 para 33). The Law Society goes further. Even where no fee is paid or shared, an online introduction service run so as to "unfairly attract work" would be improper under s 83(2)(b) and/or (h) (GN para 36). The Council has also ruled that one law practice may not demand a referral fee from another for merely brokering work (GN para 37).

These rules bind the lawyer, not the portal operator, which is why they define what lawyers can safely pay for. Three things follow from the text. First, anything that pays the portal per lead, per enquiry, per matter or as a percentage of fees is very likely a prohibited referral reward. Second, a flat, transparent, non-exclusive advertising fee is not expressly caught by anything the researchers read, and the Law Society's own paid "Find a Featured Lawyer/Law Firm" listings show that paid placement is accepted when the Society runs it ([Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.sg/find-a-featured-lawyer-law-firm/)). Third, pay-per-click sits in the middle and no guidance was found. A per-click charge correlates with enquiries, so it is riskier than a flat fee and should not be assumed safe. The "unfairly attract work" language and the touting rule, which asks whether conduct could lead to a reasonable inference of touting, are broad enough to catch a badly designed directory even under a flat fee. One 2001-era Law Gazette commentary, read only as a snippet and possibly about a predecessor rule, suggests that regular use of a third party to bring work that fails the referral criteria could support an inference of touting.

## Flat-fee listings are the safe model and lead resale is the risky one

The business-model question has a clear answer in the evidence. Singapore's closest analogues, renovation portals, sell leads. Qanvast charges designers a subscription and has them spend credits to buy leads ([Vulcan Post](https://vulcanpost.com/721133/qanvast-match-homeowners-interior-designers-singapore/)). That is the part of the Layered playbook that does not transfer, because r 39(2) says "any" consideration, which is broader than the UK position. The UK SRA allows paid lead generation if the payment is for marketing rather than referrals, requires written disclosure of any referral fee, and treats payments that vary per lead as suggesting a referral payment ([SRA marketing guidance](https://www.sra.org.uk/solicitors/guidance/marketing-public/)). The US record is sharper. Avvo's per-matter "marketing fee" product was shut down on 31 July 2018 after at least eight state bars said lawyers could not participate ([Hinshaw](https://www.hinshawlaw.com/newsroom-updates-avvo-shuts-down-its-legal-services-product-in-wake-of-ethics-opinions-warning-attorneys-not-to-participate.html), [LawNext](https://www.lawnext.com/2018/07/avvo-legal-services-shut.html)). New York's Opinion 1132 found the fees violated referral, fee-splitting and advertising rules ([NYSBA 1132](https://nysba.org/ethics-opinion-1132/)). LegalMatch, a subscription model, was held in 2019 to be an unregistered lawyer referral service under California law, and it survived by becoming State Bar-certified ([Justia](https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2019/a152442.html)). The pattern is that transaction-linked fees and platform "vouching" failed, while flat advertising and ratings survived. A subscription alone is not a guaranteed shield, since LegalMatch shows that regulators may still ask whether a directory is a referral service.

| Model | Risk for lawyers using it | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Pay per lead, per matter, or share of fees | Very high; likely breach of r 39(2) and LPA s 83(2)(d)/(e) | GN 6.1.1 para 35; PD 7.4.3; Avvo precedent |
| Pay per click | Uncertain; correlates with enquiries; no guidance found | Inference only |
| Flat equal subscription, no lead tracking, no exclusivity | Lowest of the paid options; not expressly prohibited | Law Society's own paid directory; inference |
| Free listing of verified, publicly available practice data | Lowest overall, but no revenue | Inference |
| Editorial "best lawyer" rankings or purchasable rank | High; unverifiable superlatives are flagged as misleading | PD 6.2.1 Part D |

Design choices that follow from this are concrete. Show only lawyers whose practising certificate is verified against the official register. Keep ranking neutral or clearly labelled as advertising. Do not forward "instructions", collect fees or hold client money for lawyers. Do not publish fee lists the lawyer has not approved, success rates or comparative claims. Let each lawyer approve their own listing text, since the lawyer is likely answerable for the compliance of publicity they take part in (PD 6.2.1 Part C wording). A reviews feature is a separate open question, because the researchers found no primary text on testimonials, and it also raises consumer-protection exposure for fake or incentivised reviews.

## The operator carries its own, smaller, set of risks

The PCR do not bind a non-lawyer portal directly, and no enforcement action against a directory operator was found. The operator's risks are indirect but real. The first is unauthorised practice. LPA s 33 bars an "unauthorised person" from acting as an advocate or solicitor or providing legal services, and the Law Society's example is dispatching legal demand letters through a third party (GN 6.1.1 para 32; [Law Gazette feature](https://lawgazette.com.sg/feature/unauthorised-practice-of-law-what-constitutes-acting-or-practising-as-a-solicitor/), read as a snippet that reports criminal sanctions). A portal that triages, drafts documents, or answers "what should I do about my divorce" drifts toward this line, so it should stay on directory content and generic information with disclaimers. The AI "legal concierge" model used by That Lawyer Friend ([tlfconnect.com](https://tlfconnect.com/)) sits closer to that line and warrants specific legal review.

The second risk is aiding lawyers' breaches. Whether a portal could itself be treated as touting or as abetting a lawyer's breach was not resolved, and the abetment provisions of the LPA were not checked. The third is general law. The Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act treats false claims and fake reviews as unfair practices, enforced by CCCS ([Rajah & Tann](https://www.rajahtannasia.com/viewpoints/consumer-protection-in-singapore-five-key-observations-for-businesses-in-a-heightened-enforcement-climate/)), and whether a directory's own statements fall inside it is a general-law question. The Personal Data Protection Act applies once consumers submit enquiries, which calls for consent, a data protection officer and breach notification ([Chambers 2026 guide](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/comparison/1129/18520/29027-29028-29029-29030-29031), snippet only). The researchers did not verify the Singapore advertising code. The sensible mitigations are contractual and editorial. Put PCR compliance on the lawyer, give lawyers approval over listing content, disclaim advice, avoid claims such as "we will find you the best lawyer", and require lawyers to disclose paid placement.

## Demand is real but low-ticket, and the field is already crowded

Singapore has about **1,152 law practices and 7,429 lawyers** as of May 2023, and about **80% of practices have 1-5 lawyers**, with 174 mid-sized firms and 23 large ones ([Legal Business Online](https://www.legalbusinessonline.com/sg-now-home-more-1000-law-firms-6300-lawyers-report)). That implies a customer base of roughly 900 small firms, which is a modest total for a subscription product. Consumer demand is concentrated in family law and property. Divorces and annulments reached **7,382 in 2024**, up 3.7% on 2023 ([SingStat](https://assets.app.optical.gov.sg/singstat/production/published/assets/1643130e-6f04-46c1-a6a8-11953fec431f.pdf), via search summary). HDB resales were **28,876 in 2024** ([Real Estate Asia](https://realestateasia.com/residential/news/singapore-hdb-resale-transactions-hit-27000-units-year)) and private resales **14,053** ([URA](https://www.ura.gov.sg/news/media/pr26-05/)). Law-firm marketing pages quote uncontested divorces at S$1,500-3,500 and contested ones at S$10,000-35,000 ([SingSaver](https://www.singsaver.com.sg/blog/divorce-singapore-cost-contested-uncontested-divorce)), while simple wills run S$200-400 and Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA) Form 1 S$300-700 ([A.W. Law](https://awlaw.com.sg/legal-blog/what-is-a-lasting-power-of-attorney-lpa-singapore/)). Will lawyers are therefore a weak anchor: the ticket is small and the work competes with do-it-yourself and Office of the Public Guardian options. Divorce is the strongest first vertical, with property conveyancing second.

On the supply side, lawyers face high acquisition costs, which supports the case for a cheaper channel. An agency source reports Google Ads costs above **S$15-30 per click** for terms like "divorce lawyer Singapore" ([marketingagency.sg](https://marketingagency.sg/google-ads-for-lawyers/)). That is unverified agency marketing content with no tool data behind it, so it is directional only.

Competition is the main commercial problem. The Ministry of Law's regulator search lists every practising lawyer by practice area as the authoritative register ([MinLaw LSRA](https://eservices.mlaw.gov.sg/lsra/search-lawyer-or-law-firm/)), and the Law Society sells paid featured listings ([Law Society](https://www.lawsociety.org.sg/find-a-lawyer/)). Private players include SingaporeLegalAdvice.com, founded in 2011 and self-reporting 200,000+ monthly visitors ([Asia Law Portal](https://asialawportal.com/singaporelegaladvice-com-a-one-stop-platform-for-lawyers-and-legal-consumers/)), and The Lawyer Guide, which forwards requests to lawyers who reply with non-binding offers ([thelawyerguide.sg](https://thelawyerguide.sg/)). That is a lead-forwarding design and may face the referral issue above, though no sanction against it was found. Others are SgFindLawyer ([sgfindlawyer.com](https://sgfindlawyer.com/)), That Lawyer Friend, which markets itself as vetted with "no pay-to-be-featured" and no cut of fees, and SingaporeFREELegalAdvice ([singaporefreelegaladvice.com](https://www.singaporefreelegaladvice.com/)). No dominant consumer brand is evident, so the gap is in verified data, pricing transparency and user experience rather than in raw listings. No Singapore survey of how consumers actually find lawyers was found. Discovery via Google, property agents and word of mouth is assumed, not demonstrated.

## What the evidence cannot yet support

The researchers' web access was limited, and the report's confidence should be set accordingly. The egress proxy blocked the Singapore Statutes Online site, the Law Society main site and the Law Gazette, among others. The PCR 2015 and LPA text was never read directly. The only verified primary text comes from three Law Society documents dated 2018-2019 (GN 6.1.1, PD 6.2.1, PD 7.4.3), which pre-date the LPA 2020 Revised Edition and any later amendment. Rule numbers are therefore unreliable. The notes themselves cite the touting rule variously as r 39, r 39/43(4) and r 43(4), and snippet-sourced numbers for rr 38-47 are unverified. Treat every rule and section number in this report as a pointer, not a citation to rely on.

The central legal conclusion is an inference. No ruling was found that approves or rejects a flat-fee directory, pay-per-click, or any third-party pay-per-lead platform in Singapore, and no Law Society guidance specifically on third-party directories, comparison sites or Google Ads was found. No disciplinary decision on lawyer advertising or directories could be retrieved. Rules on testimonials, awards, rankings and fee advertising are also unread. Commercial data is similarly soft. Competitor pricing, how each competitor earns money, and traffic beyond one self-reported figure are unknown. The renovation-portal revenue figures conflict and are unusable. Demand data is missing for criminal, personal injury, employment and probate work. Fee ranges come from firm marketing pages, and the cost-per-lead arithmetic in the research notes is an unsourced assumption. Finally, no keyword volume data was available, because the SEO tooling failed to connect.

## Verdict and next steps

The verdict is conditional go. Build a flat-fee, verified-listing directory focused first on divorce, then conveyancing, and treat the Layered lead-purchase model as off the table until a Singapore lawyer or the Law Society says otherwise. The downside is bounded if the portal never takes per-lead money, never touches instructions or fees, and never gives advice. The upside is limited by a customer base of about 900 small firms, low-ticket wills, and incumbents with years of SEO. A directory that only repackages the official register will not win; it needs verified credentials, transparent pricing and a better consumer experience.

Recommended steps, in order. First, before spending on product, commission a written opinion from a Singapore practitioner on four questions: whether a flat subscription is safe under r 39(2) and LPA s 83(2); whether pay-per-click is acceptable; whether the portal itself risks touting or abetment exposure; and which current rule numbers apply. Second, ask the Law Society Ethics Committee or the Ministry of Law for written guidance on third-party directories, which no source located. Third, pull the primary text from Singapore Statutes Online (PCR 2015 Part 5 as amended, the LPA 2020 Revised Edition ss 33 and 83) and check recent Law Gazette "Ethics in Practice" columns. Fourth, validate demand cheaply: use Keyword Planner or Ahrefs for Singapore legal searches, and interview 10-15 small-firm lawyers about what they pay for marketing and whether they would pay a flat fee. Fifth, review competitor pricing by approaching the existing directories as a prospective advertiser. Sixth, draft lawyer terms that place PCR compliance on the lawyer, give approval over listing text and prohibit fee claims, testimonials and superlatives until the rules are confirmed, and appoint a data protection officer under the PDPA.

## Conclusion

The renovation-portal analogy fails at exactly the point that produces revenue. Qanvast earns by selling leads, while Singapore's referral rules appear to forbid lawyers from paying for them, so a lawyer portal is a different business that earns by selling advertising at flat prices. That business is small, since about 900 small firms are the realistic base and wills and powers of attorney are cheap services, which makes divorce and conveyancing the commercial core. The most useful next move is not building anything but obtaining the written legal opinion, because every material conclusion here that concerns rule numbers, touting, and the safety of pay-per-click rests on inference. If that opinion confirms that a flat-fee model is safe, the remaining question is commercial: whether verified data and better UX can beat incumbents that already hold search traffic.
